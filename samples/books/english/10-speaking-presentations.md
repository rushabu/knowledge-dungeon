# Speaking and Presentation Skills

Good ideas need to be heard. In college you will present projects, take part in group discussions and face viva examinations. At work you will attend interviews, pitch ideas and lead meetings. Spoken communication can feel more stressful than writing, because it happens in real time, but it is a skill like any other: it improves with preparation and practice. This final chapter covers the basics of clear speaking, presentations, group discussions and interviews.

## Elements of Effective Speaking

### Clarity and Pronunciation

You do not need a "foreign" accent to speak English well. Indian English is a respected variety of English. What matters is being **clearly understood**.

- **Speak at a moderate pace.** Nervous speakers rush. Aim for about 130 to 150 words per minute.
- **Articulate** word endings and consonants clearly, especially in technical terms.
- **Stress the right syllable**: *de-VE-lop*, *PHO-to-graph* but *pho-TO-gra-phy*, *e-CO-no-my*. Wrong stress causes more misunderstanding than accent does.
- **Pause** at the end of ideas. Pauses give listeners time to think and make you sound confident.

### Voice

- **Volume:** loud enough for the person at the back of the room to hear.
- **Pitch and intonation:** vary your tone; a flat, monotonous voice loses attention. Rising intonation signals a question; falling intonation signals a statement or conclusion.
- **Emphasis:** stress key words to highlight important points: "This method is *three times* faster."

### Fillers

Fillers like *um, uh, like, basically, actually* and *you know* distract listeners when overused. Replace them with a brief, silent pause. Recording yourself is the fastest way to notice your habits.

## Non-Verbal Communication

Research on communication consistently finds that listeners judge speakers heavily on non-verbal signals.

- **Eye contact:** look at different people in the audience for a few seconds each. In interviews, maintain natural eye contact with the interviewer.
- **Posture:** stand or sit straight, with shoulders relaxed. Avoid leaning on the table or crossing your arms.
- **Gestures:** use open, natural hand gestures to emphasise points. Avoid fidgeting with pens, hair or your phone.
- **Facial expression:** a genuine smile at the start builds rapport.
- **Movement:** move with purpose; do not pace back and forth.

> **Key idea:** Confidence is largely *shown* through body language. Even when nervous inside, a steady posture, eye contact and a calm pace make you appear confident, and often make you feel more confident too.

## Listening Skills

Communication is two-way. **Active listening** means:

- giving full attention without planning your reply;
- showing interest through nods and brief responses ("I see", "That's a good point");
- asking clarifying questions ("Do you mean...?");
- paraphrasing to confirm understanding ("So you are suggesting that..."); and
- not interrupting.

Good listeners make better speakers, especially in discussions and interviews.

## Presentations

### Planning

1. **Know your audience:** What do they already know? What do they need? A presentation on your ML project would be very different for classmates, professors and a non-technical audience.
2. **Define your purpose:** to inform, persuade, demonstrate or update.
3. **Choose your key message:** if the audience remembers only one sentence, what should it be?
4. **Research and select content:** include only what supports your message. Three main points are easier to follow than ten.

### Structure

Use a simple, proven structure: *tell them what you will tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them.*

- **Opening (about 10 percent):** grab attention with a question, a surprising fact, a short story or a demonstration. Introduce yourself and the topic, and preview the main points.
- **Body (about 80 percent):** present your main points in a logical order, with evidence and examples for each. Use **signposting** language so listeners know where you are:
  - "First, I'll explain the problem."
  - "Moving on to the results..."
  - "This brings me to my final point."
- **Closing (about 10 percent):** summarise the main points, restate the key message, and end strongly: a call to action, a memorable line or a look ahead. Then invite questions.

### Designing Slides

Slides should **support** you, not replace you.

- **One idea per slide,** with a clear headline stating the point, such as "Accuracy improved by 12 percent", rather than just "Results".
- **Minimal text:** use the "6 x 6" guideline as a maximum (no more than six bullet points of about six words each), or better, use visuals.
- **Readable fonts:** at least 24 point for body text, with high contrast between text and background.
- **Visuals:** charts, diagrams and images explain faster than text. Label axes and keep charts simple.
- **Consistency:** the same fonts, colours and layout throughout.
- **Avoid** reading slides aloud word for word, flashy animations and cluttered templates.

### Delivering the Presentation

- **Rehearse aloud** several times, ideally in front of friends, and time yourself.
- **Know your opening and closing** almost by heart; they are when nerves are strongest and attention highest.
- Use **notes or cue cards** with keywords, not full sentences.
- **Face the audience,** not the screen.
- **Check the technology** beforehand: projector, clicker, internet and backup copies of your file.
- For a **live demo,** prepare a recorded backup in case something fails.

### Handling Questions

- **Listen to the whole question** without interrupting.
- **Repeat or paraphrase** it if the room is large, so everyone hears it and you gain a moment to think.
- **Answer briefly and directly.**
- If you do not know, say so honestly: "That's a good question. I haven't tested that yet, but I'll look into it." Never bluff.
- **Stay calm and polite** even with challenging questions.

### Managing Nervousness

Nearly everyone feels nervous before speaking in public. Useful techniques:

- **Prepare thoroughly:** preparation is the best cure for anxiety.
- **Breathe slowly and deeply** before starting: breathe in for four counts and out for six.
- **Reframe** nervousness as excitement; the physical feelings are almost the same.
- **Focus on the audience and the message,** not on yourself.
- **Start strong:** a well-rehearsed opening builds momentum.
- **Accept small mistakes;** the audience rarely notices them as much as you do.

## Group Discussions

Many companies and colleges use **group discussions (GDs)** to assess communication, teamwork and leadership. A group of six to twelve people discusses a topic for 10 to 20 minutes while evaluators observe.

### What Evaluators Look For

- **Content:** relevant, logical points with examples and facts.
- **Communication:** clarity, fluency and confidence.
- **Listening and teamwork:** building on others' points and respecting different views.
- **Leadership:** initiating, guiding the discussion back on track, summarising.

### Tips for Group Discussions

1. **Initiate if you can** with a strong, relevant opening (define the topic, give context or a key fact), but only if you are prepared.
2. **Make quality contributions,** not the most contributions. Three thoughtful points beat ten repetitive ones.
3. **Use polite language to agree and disagree:**
   - "I agree with Priya, and I'd like to add that..."
   - "That's an interesting point, but I see it differently because..."
   - "Could we also consider the impact on small businesses?"
4. **Bring in quieter members:** "Rohan, what do you think?" This shows leadership.
5. **Never shout, interrupt aggressively or attack people personally.**
6. **Summarise at the end** if the moderator asks or the group has not concluded.

## Interviews

### Before the Interview

- **Research the organisation:** its products, values and recent news.
- **Study the job description** and match your skills and projects to it.
- **Prepare examples** of your achievements using the **STAR method**:
  - **S**ituation: the context.
  - **T**ask: your responsibility.
  - **A**ction: what you did.
  - **R**esult: the outcome, with numbers if possible.
- **Practise common questions** aloud (see below).
- **Prepare questions** to ask the interviewer.

### Common Interview Questions

| Question | What they want to know |
|---|---|
| Tell me about yourself. | A brief professional summary: education, key skills, a highlight, and why you are here (about one minute). |
| Why do you want to work here? | That you have researched them and your goals match. |
| What are your strengths? | Relevant strengths backed by examples. |
| What is your biggest weakness? | Self-awareness and effort to improve (choose a real, non-critical weakness and explain what you are doing about it). |
| Describe a challenge you faced. | Problem-solving; answer with STAR. |
| Where do you see yourself in five years? | Ambition that fits the role. |
| Do you have any questions for us? | Interest and preparation; always ask one or two thoughtful questions. |

### During the Interview

- **Arrive early** (or log in early for online interviews, having tested your camera, microphone and internet connection).
- **Dress appropriately** for the organisation.
- **Greet politely,** with a smile and a firm handshake where appropriate.
- **Listen carefully** and take a moment to think before answering.
- **Be honest;** it is fine to say you do not know something and explain how you would find out.
- **Show enthusiasm** for the role.
- **Thank the interviewers** at the end, and consider sending a short thank-you email afterwards.

## Online Communication

Video calls are now routine for classes, interviews and meetings.

- Test your **audio and video** in advance and use headphones if possible.
- Choose a **quiet, well-lit place** with a neutral background; light should face you, not come from behind.
- Look at the **camera** when speaking to create eye contact.
- **Mute** when not speaking in group calls, and use the chat or "raise hand" features politely.
- **Dress** as you would in person.

## Summary

- Clear speaking depends on pace, articulation, correct word stress, pauses and a varied voice, not on accent.
- Non-verbal signals such as eye contact, posture and gestures strongly affect how you are perceived.
- Active listening improves every conversation.
- Plan presentations around the audience and one key message; structure them with an opening, a signposted body and a strong close.
- Design simple, visual slides; rehearse aloud; handle questions honestly.
- In group discussions, contribute quality points, respect others and help the group reach conclusions.
- Prepare for interviews with research and STAR stories, and communicate professionally online.

## Practice Questions

1. Record yourself giving a two-minute talk on your favourite subject. List your fillers and pace problems.
2. Write an attention-grabbing opening for a presentation on "Cyber safety for college students".
3. Suggest three polite phrases for disagreeing in a group discussion.
4. Use the STAR method to answer: "Tell me about a time you worked in a team to meet a deadline."
5. Redesign this slide title to state a point: "Survey Results".
