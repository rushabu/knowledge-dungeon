# Writing Paragraphs and Essays

Clear writing is clear thinking made visible. Whether you are answering an exam question, writing a lab report, drafting a statement of purpose or posting on a professional network, the same principles apply: know your point, organise your ideas, support them with evidence, and express them simply. This chapter builds from the paragraph to the full essay, and covers the writing process from planning to proofreading.

## The Paragraph

A **paragraph** is a group of sentences that develops **one main idea**. A good paragraph has three qualities:

- **Unity:** every sentence relates to the main idea.
- **Coherence:** sentences follow a logical order and connect smoothly.
- **Adequate development:** the idea is explained and supported enough to convince the reader.

### The Structure of a Paragraph

1. **Topic sentence:** states the main idea, usually at the beginning.
2. **Supporting sentences:** explain, give reasons, provide examples, facts or evidence.
3. **Concluding sentence:** sums up or links to the next paragraph (optional in short pieces).

### The PEEL Method

A reliable pattern for body paragraphs in essays:

- **P**oint: state your claim.
- **E**vidence: give facts, data, examples or quotations.
- **E**xplain: show how the evidence supports your point.
- **L**ink: connect back to the main argument or to the next paragraph.

Example:

> **(P)** Public libraries remain important in the digital age. **(E)** A 2024 survey found that more than half of library visitors in large Indian cities used them for free internet access and quiet study space. **(E)** This shows that libraries provide resources that many students cannot afford at home. **(L)** Therefore, cutting library funding would widen the gap between students of different incomes.

> **Key idea:** One paragraph, one idea. If you find yourself starting a new idea, start a new paragraph.

### Achieving Coherence

- Use **transition words**: *first, moreover, however, for example, as a result, finally*.
- **Repeat key terms** or use pronouns and synonyms to link sentences.
- Follow a clear **order**: chronological (time), spatial (place), order of importance, general to specific, or problem to solution.

## Types of Paragraphs and Essays

| Type | Purpose | Example topic |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Paint a picture with sensory detail | A monsoon evening in Kerala |
| Narrative | Tell a story or sequence of events | My first day at college |
| Expository | Explain or inform objectively | How UPI payments work |
| Argumentative / persuasive | Take a position and convince | Should attendance be compulsory? |
| Compare and contrast | Show similarities and differences | Online vs classroom learning |
| Cause and effect | Explain why something happens and its results | Causes and effects of air pollution |

## The Structure of an Essay

Most academic essays follow a three-part structure.

### 1. Introduction

The introduction should:

- **hook** the reader: a striking fact, a question, a short anecdote or a quotation;
- give **context**: a little background on the topic; and
- end with a **thesis statement**: one or two sentences stating your main argument or the essay's purpose.

A strong thesis is **specific** and **arguable**.

- Weak: "Social media has advantages and disadvantages." (too vague)
- Strong: "Although social media helps students collaborate, its constant notifications reduce concentration, so colleges should teach digital self-discipline."

### 2. Body Paragraphs

Each body paragraph develops **one** supporting point for the thesis, typically using PEEL. A short essay usually has three body paragraphs. Order them logically, for example building up to your strongest point.

In an argumentative essay, include a **counter-argument** and **rebuttal**: acknowledge the opposing view fairly, then explain why your position is still stronger. This makes your argument more convincing, not weaker.

### 3. Conclusion

The conclusion should:

- **restate the thesis** in fresh words (do not copy it);
- **summarise** the main points briefly; and
- end with a **final thought**: a recommendation, a call to action, a wider implication or a question for the future.

Do not introduce completely new arguments in the conclusion.

## The Writing Process

Good writing is rarely produced in one go. Follow these stages.

### 1. Understand the Task

Read the question carefully. Identify **instruction words**:

- *Describe:* give the features.
- *Explain:* give reasons and how something works.
- *Discuss:* examine different sides.
- *Evaluate / assess:* judge strengths and weaknesses and reach a conclusion.
- *Compare:* show similarities (and often differences).
- *Justify:* give reasons for a position.

### 2. Brainstorm

Generate ideas freely: list points, draw a mind map, or free-write for five minutes without stopping.

### 3. Plan and Outline

Choose your thesis and three or four main points, and arrange them logically. A quick outline saves time and prevents rambling.

```
Thesis: Colleges should adopt a four-day class week.
  1. Improves student well-being (evidence: stress survey)
  2. Frees time for internships and projects
  3. Counter-argument: less teaching time -> rebuttal: longer, focused sessions
Conclusion: pilot the idea for one semester
```

### 4. Draft

Write the first version without worrying too much about perfection. Focus on getting your ideas down in the planned order.

### 5. Revise

Revising means improving the **content and organisation**:

- Is the thesis clear? Does every paragraph support it?
- Is the evidence convincing? Is anything missing or irrelevant?
- Is the order logical? Are transitions smooth?

### 6. Edit and Proofread

Editing focuses on **sentences and words**; proofreading on **surface errors**.

- Cut unnecessary words. "Due to the fact that" becomes "because"; "in order to" becomes "to"; "at this point in time" becomes "now".
- Replace vague words (*things, stuff, very good*) with precise ones.
- Check grammar, spelling, punctuation, agreement and tense.
- Read aloud; awkward sentences become obvious.

> **Key idea:** Separate writing from editing. Trying to write perfect sentences on the first draft slows you down and blocks your ideas.

## Style: Writing Clearly

1. **Prefer short, simple words**: *use* rather than *utilise*, *help* rather than *facilitate*.
2. **Keep sentences focused:** aim for an average of 15 to 20 words, with variety.
3. **Use the active voice** in most cases: "The team designed the app", not "The app was designed by the team".
4. **Be specific:** "Sales rose by 18 percent in March" beats "Sales increased a lot recently".
5. **Avoid clichés and filler:** "in today's modern world", "since time immemorial", "last but not the least".
6. **Keep a consistent tone:** formal for academic work (no slang, contractions or text-speak).

## Academic Integrity

Using other people's words or ideas without credit is **plagiarism**, and colleges treat it seriously.

- **Quote** exact words in quotation marks and cite the source.
- **Paraphrase** by restating ideas completely in your own words and sentence structure, and still cite the source.
- **Summarise** the main points briefly in your own words, with a citation.
- Use a consistent citation style (APA, MLA, IEEE) as your department requires.

AI writing tools can help you brainstorm or check grammar, but submitting AI-generated text as your own work is usually treated as academic misconduct. Follow your college's rules.

## Writing Under Exam Conditions

1. Spend about **10 percent** of the time planning, **80 percent** writing and **10 percent** checking.
2. Answer the exact question, using its keywords in your thesis.
3. Use clear paragraphs; examiners reward structure.
4. Keep an eye on the word limit; writing more is not always better.
5. Leave time to proofread for obvious errors.

## A Sample Short Essay Outline

**Topic:** "Should mobile phones be allowed in classrooms?"

- **Introduction:** hook with a statistic on smartphone use among students; thesis: phones should be allowed only for supervised learning activities.
- **Body 1:** educational benefits: instant research, learning apps, accessibility tools.
- **Body 2:** distractions and cheating risks, with evidence on attention.
- **Body 3 (counter-argument and rebuttal):** a full ban is simpler; but clear rules and teacher control preserve the benefits while limiting harm.
- **Conclusion:** restate the balanced position; recommend a clear classroom phone policy.

## Summary

- A paragraph develops one idea with a topic sentence, support and, optionally, a conclusion; aim for unity, coherence and development.
- PEEL (point, evidence, explain, link) structures strong body paragraphs.
- Essays have an introduction with a hook and a specific, arguable thesis, body paragraphs that support it, and a conclusion that restates and reflects.
- Follow the writing process: understand, brainstorm, outline, draft, revise, edit and proofread.
- Write clearly: simple words, active voice, specific details, no filler.
- Credit sources to avoid plagiarism; plan your time carefully in exams.
