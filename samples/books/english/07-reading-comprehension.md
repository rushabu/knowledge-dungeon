# Reading Comprehension

Reading is the foundation of every other language skill. You read textbooks, research papers, emails, instructions, news and, in placement tests and competitive exams, timed comprehension passages. Good readers do not simply read faster; they read **actively**, with a purpose, adjusting their approach to the text. This chapter explains reading strategies, how to handle different kinds of questions, and how to improve speed without losing understanding.

## Why Comprehension Is More Than Reading Words

You can read every word of a paragraph and still not understand it. Comprehension means grasping:

- the **main idea**: what the passage is mainly about;
- the **supporting details**: facts, examples and reasons;
- the **structure**: how ideas connect;
- the **author's purpose and tone**: why and how it was written; and
- **implied meanings**: what the author suggests but does not say directly.

> **Key idea:** Active readers constantly ask questions: What is this about? Why is the author telling me this? How does this connect to what came before?

## Reading Techniques

### Skimming

**Skimming** means reading quickly to get the general idea. Read the title, headings, the first and last paragraphs, and the first sentence of each paragraph (which is often the **topic sentence**). Use skimming to preview a chapter, decide whether an article is relevant, or get an overview of a comprehension passage before reading it closely.

### Scanning

**Scanning** means searching quickly for specific information: a date, a name, a number or a keyword. Your eyes move rapidly over the text until they find what you need. Use scanning to answer factual questions or to find a timing in a train timetable.

### Intensive Reading

**Intensive reading** means reading carefully and slowly for full understanding: every sentence, every argument. Use it for textbooks, contracts, instructions and the key parts of an exam passage.

### Extensive Reading

**Extensive reading** means reading large amounts for pleasure or general knowledge: novels, magazines, long articles. It builds vocabulary, fluency and general knowledge over time.

## The SQ3R Method

**SQ3R** is a well-known study-reading method:

1. **Survey:** skim the headings, summaries and diagrams to get an overview.
2. **Question:** turn headings into questions. "Causes of Inflation" becomes "What causes inflation?"
3. **Read:** read actively to answer your questions.
4. **Recite:** close the book and say or write the main points in your own words.
5. **Review:** go over your notes later to strengthen memory.

This method turns passive reading into active learning.

## Understanding Paragraph Structure

Most well-written paragraphs have:

- a **topic sentence** stating the main idea, usually at the start;
- **supporting sentences** giving explanations, evidence and examples; and
- sometimes a **concluding sentence** that sums up or links to the next paragraph.

**Signal words** (transitions) reveal how ideas relate:

| Relationship | Signal words |
|---|---|
| Addition | also, moreover, furthermore, in addition |
| Contrast | however, but, although, on the other hand, whereas |
| Cause and effect | because, therefore, as a result, consequently |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as |
| Sequence | first, then, next, finally |
| Conclusion | in conclusion, thus, overall, to sum up |

Words like *however* and *but* are especially important: what comes after them is often the author's real point.

## Types of Comprehension Questions

### 1. Main Idea Questions

"What is the passage mainly about?" or "Which title best suits the passage?"

Strategy: look for the idea that covers the *whole* passage. Wrong options are often too narrow (covering one paragraph) or too broad (going beyond the passage).

### 2. Detail (Factual) Questions

"According to the passage, when was the bridge built?"

Strategy: scan for keywords from the question and read the surrounding sentences carefully. The answer is stated directly, often in slightly different words (a **paraphrase**).

### 3. Inference Questions

"What can be inferred from the passage?" or "The author implies that..."

Strategy: the answer is not stated directly but must follow logically from the text. Avoid options that go too far or rely on outside knowledge. A correct inference is *supported by* the passage, not merely *possible*.

### 4. Vocabulary in Context

"The word *mitigate* in paragraph 2 most nearly means..."

Strategy: reread the sentence and substitute each option. The right answer fits the context, even if another option is a more common meaning of the word.

### 5. Author's Purpose and Tone

"Why does the author mention the survey?" or "The tone of the passage is..."

Common purposes: to inform, persuade, explain, entertain, criticise or compare. Tone words include *objective, critical, optimistic, sarcastic, nostalgic, cautious, enthusiastic* and *neutral*.

### 6. Structure and Function

"How is the passage organised?" (problem and solution, cause and effect, chronological, comparison) or "What is the function of the third paragraph?"

### 7. True/False/Not Given

Some tests ask whether a statement is true, false or **not given**. "Not given" means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it. Do not use your own knowledge.

> **Key idea:** In comprehension tests, the passage is the only source of truth. An option that is true in real life but not supported by the passage is wrong.

## A Worked Example

> *Urban gardens are spreading across Indian cities. On rooftops in Bengaluru and balconies in Mumbai, residents are growing vegetables, herbs and flowers. Supporters argue that such gardens reduce household expenses and improve mental well-being. However, critics point out that without proper waterproofing, rooftop gardens can damage buildings. Experts therefore recommend professional advice before starting large rooftop projects.*

- **Main idea:** urban gardening is growing in Indian cities and has benefits but needs care.
- **Detail:** Where are balcony gardens mentioned? Mumbai.
- **Inference:** Some rooftop gardens have probably caused building damage. (Supported by the critics' concern.)
- **Vocabulary:** "proper" most nearly means "suitable" or "correct".
- **Purpose:** to inform readers about a trend and its pros and cons.
- **Tone:** balanced and objective.
- **Signal word:** "However" introduces the opposing view; "therefore" introduces the conclusion.

## Strategies for Timed Tests

1. **Read the questions first** (or at least skim them) so you know what to look for.
2. **Skim the passage** for the main idea and structure, marking key words.
3. **Answer detail questions** by scanning back to the relevant lines.
4. **Eliminate wrong options:** extreme words (*always, never, all, completely*) are often wrong; options that are true but irrelevant are wrong.
5. **Manage time:** do not get stuck on one hard question. Mark it and return later.
6. **For inference questions,** choose the option that needs the smallest logical step from the text.

## Reading Critically

At college level, understanding is not enough; you must also **evaluate**.

- **Fact vs opinion:** "The dam was completed in 2010" is a fact that can be checked. "The dam was a waste of money" is an opinion.
- **Evidence:** Does the author support claims with data, examples or expert sources?
- **Bias:** Does the author present only one side? Who wrote it, and why?
- **Logic:** Do the conclusions follow from the evidence?
- **Currency:** Is the information up to date?

These questions are essential when reading news and social media, where misinformation spreads quickly.

## Improving Reading Speed

- **Read in chunks:** train your eyes to take in groups of words rather than one word at a time.
- **Reduce subvocalisation:** try not to "say" every word silently in your head, which limits you to speaking speed.
- **Avoid regression:** resist rereading every line; reread only when truly necessary.
- **Preview first:** knowing the structure lets you read faster.
- **Practise regularly** with timed reading, gradually increasing difficulty.

Speed should never cost comprehension. The goal is *efficient* reading: fast where the text is easy, slow where it matters.

## Making Notes While Reading

- Underline or highlight sparingly: only key ideas, not whole paragraphs.
- Write short notes in the margin: "definition", "example", "author disagrees".
- Summarise each section in one or two sentences in your own words.
- Use **mind maps** to show how ideas connect, or the **Cornell method**: divide your page into notes, key questions, and a summary at the bottom.

## Summary

- Comprehension means understanding main ideas, details, structure, purpose, tone and implications.
- Skim for the general idea, scan for specific facts, read intensively for depth and extensively for fluency.
- SQ3R (survey, question, read, recite, review) makes study reading active.
- Topic sentences and signal words reveal structure.
- Learn the main question types: main idea, detail, inference, vocabulary, purpose and tone, structure, and true/false/not given.
- In tests, rely only on the passage and eliminate extreme or irrelevant options.
- Read critically: separate fact from opinion, and check evidence and bias.
