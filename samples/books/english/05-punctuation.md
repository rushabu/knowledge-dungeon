# Punctuation and Mechanics

Punctuation marks are the traffic signals of writing. They tell the reader when to pause, when to stop, and how ideas relate to each other. Misplaced punctuation can change meaning completely: "Let's eat, Grandma!" is an invitation, while "Let's eat Grandma!" is a horror story. This chapter covers each major punctuation mark, along with capitalisation, numbers and abbreviations.

## The Full Stop (Period)

Use a full stop:

- at the end of a statement or a polite command: "The meeting is at noon." "Please submit your forms."
- in some abbreviations: *e.g.*, *i.e.*, *etc.*, *Dr.*, *Mr.* (British style often omits the full stop in *Dr* and *Mr*).

Do not add a second full stop when a sentence ends with an abbreviation: "Bring pens, pencils, erasers, etc."

## The Question Mark

Use a question mark after a **direct** question: "Where is the library?"

Do not use one after an **indirect** question: "She asked where the library was."

## The Exclamation Mark

Use an exclamation mark to show strong emotion or emphasis: "What a fantastic result!" Use it sparingly. In formal and academic writing, avoid it almost entirely, and never use several together ("Amazing!!!").

## The Comma

The comma is the most frequently used, and misused, punctuation mark. It marks a short pause and separates parts of a sentence.

### 1. In Lists

Separate three or more items: "We need paper, ink, and staples." The comma before *and* in a list is called the **Oxford (serial) comma**. It is optional in many styles, but it can prevent confusion: "I thanked my parents, Sachin Tendulkar and Lata Mangeshkar" sounds as if my parents are Sachin and Lata. "I thanked my parents, Sachin Tendulkar, and Lata Mangeshkar" does not. Be consistent either way.

### 2. Before a Coordinating Conjunction Joining Two Independent Clauses

"I wanted to attend the seminar, but I had an exam." (Use FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.)

If the clauses are short and closely linked, the comma can be omitted: "She sang and he danced."

### 3. After Introductory Elements

"However, the results were disappointing." "After the lecture, we went to the canteen." "If you need help, call me."

### 4. Around Non-Essential Information

"My sister, who lives in Chennai, is a lawyer." "Mumbai, the financial capital of India, is very crowded." If you can remove the words without changing the basic meaning, set them off with commas.

### 5. With Direct Address

"Rahul, please close the window." "Thank you, sir." Missing this comma causes the "Let's eat Grandma" problem.

### 6. In Dates, Addresses and Numbers

"On 15 August 1947, India became independent." "She lives in Andheri, Mumbai." In the Indian numbering system, commas mark lakhs and crores: 1,00,000 (one lakh); in the international system, the same number is 100,000.

### 7. With Quotations

"She said, 'I will be late.'"

### Common Comma Mistakes

- **Comma splice:** joining two sentences with only a comma. "I finished the report, I emailed it." Fix: use a full stop, a semicolon, or a conjunction.
- **Comma between subject and verb:** "The students who attended the workshop, received certificates." Remove the comma.
- **Comma before *that* in defining clauses:** "The book, that I borrowed, is overdue." Correct: "The book that I borrowed is overdue."

> **Key idea:** Do not put a comma wherever you would pause when speaking. Commas follow grammatical rules; learn the seven uses above and you will get most commas right.

## The Semicolon

A semicolon connects two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction:

- "The exam was difficult; most students found the last section confusing."

It is also used before transition words such as *however*, *therefore*, *moreover* and *otherwise* when they join two clauses:

- "The trains were delayed; therefore, the meeting was postponed."

And it separates items in a list when the items themselves contain commas:

- "The finalists came from Pune, Maharashtra; Kochi, Kerala; and Indore, Madhya Pradesh."

## The Colon

A colon introduces something that explains, lists or completes what came before it. The part before a colon should usually be a complete sentence.

- A list: "You will need three things: a pen, a calculator and your admit card."
- An explanation: "There was only one problem: nobody had the key."
- A quotation or formal statement: "The principal's message was simple: work hard and be kind."
- Times, ratios and titles: *10:30 am*, *a ratio of 3:1*, *English Grammar: A Practical Guide*.
- After the greeting in a formal letter (American style): "Dear Sir:"

Do not put a colon directly after a verb or preposition: "The ingredients are: flour, sugar and eggs" is wrong. Write "The ingredients are flour, sugar and eggs."

## The Apostrophe

The apostrophe has two jobs.

### 1. Possession

- Singular noun: add *'s*: *the student's book*, *James's car* (or *James' car*).
- Plural noun ending in *s*: add only the apostrophe: *the students' books*, *the teachers' room*.
- Irregular plural: add *'s*: *the children's park*, *women's rights*.

### 2. Contractions

The apostrophe replaces missing letters: *do not = don't*, *it is = it's*, *they are = they're*, *I would = I'd*. Contractions are fine in informal writing but should usually be avoided in formal and academic writing.

### Famous Confusions

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| its | belonging to it | The company changed its logo. |
| it's | it is / it has | It's raining. |
| your | belonging to you | Is this your bag? |
| you're | you are | You're late. |
| their | belonging to them | Their results are out. |
| there | a place / there is | Put it there. |
| they're | they are | They're coming. |
| whose | belonging to whom | Whose phone is this? |
| who's | who is / who has | Who's calling? |

Never use an apostrophe to make a plain plural: "Apple's for sale" should be "Apples for sale".

## Quotation Marks

Use quotation marks for direct speech and exact quotations: She said, "The deadline is Friday." Some styles use double quotes and some single quotes; be consistent. Use the other kind for a quotation within a quotation.

Quotation marks can also mark titles of short works (articles, poems, chapters) and words used in a special sense: What does "agile" mean in this context?

In Indian and British practice, full stops and commas usually go inside the quotation marks when they belong to the quoted words.

## The Hyphen and the Dash

### Hyphen (-)

The hyphen joins words.

- **Compound adjectives before a noun:** *a well-known author*, *a part-time job*, *a three-year course*. But "The author is well known." (no hyphen after the noun)
- **Numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine** written as words.
- **Some prefixes:** *ex-captain*, *self-confidence*, *re-elect*.

### Dashes

The **en dash** (–) shows ranges: *pages 10–25*, *2020–2024*. The **em dash** (—) marks a strong break or adds emphasis: "The answer—if there is one—lies in the data." In informal writing, a spaced hyphen is often used instead. Do not overuse dashes in formal writing.

## Brackets (Parentheses)

Round brackets hold extra information that is not essential: "The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) regulates banks." They are also used for abbreviations introduced after the full term, as in the example. Square brackets show words added to a quotation by someone else.

## Capitalisation

Use capital letters for:

- the first word of a sentence;
- the pronoun *I*;
- proper nouns: names of people, places, organisations, days, months, festivals, languages and nationalities: *Anil, Kolkata, Infosys, Tuesday, March, Holi, Marathi, Indian*;
- titles before names: *Professor Rao*, *Doctor Mehta* (but "the professor", "a doctor");
- major words in titles of books, films and articles: *The Discovery of India*;
- abbreviations and acronyms: *ISRO, UNESCO, CEO*.

Do not capitalise seasons (*summer*), general subjects (*mathematics*, but *English*, because it is a language), or directions (*north*, but *North India* as a region).

## Numbers and Abbreviations

- In formal writing, spell out numbers from one to nine (or up to one hundred in some styles) and use figures for larger numbers: "three students", "250 participants".
- Always use figures for dates, times, percentages, money and measurements: *5 June*, *4:30 pm*, *12 percent*, *Rs 500*, *3 km*.
- Do not start a sentence with a figure: write "Twenty students attended", not "20 students attended".
- Write out a term in full the first time, with its abbreviation in brackets, then use the abbreviation: "Artificial intelligence (AI) is changing industries. AI tools..."
- *e.g.* means "for example"; *i.e.* means "that is". "Bring a fruit, e.g. an apple" (one example). "Bring your ID, i.e. your college card" (exactly this).

## Summary

- Full stops end statements; question marks follow direct questions only; exclamation marks are rare in formal writing.
- Commas separate list items, join clauses with FANBOYS, follow introductory elements, surround non-essential information and mark direct address.
- Semicolons join related independent clauses; colons introduce lists and explanations after a complete sentence.
- Apostrophes show possession and contractions; *its* and *it's* are different words.
- Hyphens join compound adjectives before nouns; dashes show ranges and breaks.
- Capitalise proper nouns and titles; follow conventions for numbers and abbreviations.

2. Correct the comma splice in two different ways: "The lab was closed, we went to the library."
3. Choose the right word: "(Its / It's) clear that the team has lost (its / it's) confidence."
