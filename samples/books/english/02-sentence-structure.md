# Sentence Structure

Words become meaningful only when they are arranged into sentences. A sentence expresses a complete thought, and English sentences follow patterns. Once you know these patterns, you can write sentences that are clear, varied and correct, and you can spot problems like fragments and run-on sentences in your own work.

## What Is a Sentence?

A **sentence** is a group of words that expresses a complete thought. It begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark. Every complete sentence has at least:

- a **subject**: who or what the sentence is about, and
- a **predicate**: what the subject does or is, which always contains a verb.

In "The students submitted their projects", the subject is *The students* and the predicate is *submitted their projects*.

## Parts of a Sentence

- **Subject:** the doer or the topic. *Priya* laughed.
- **Verb:** the action or state. Priya *laughed*.
- **Object:** receives the action.
  - A **direct object** answers "what?" or "whom?": She wrote *a letter*.
  - An **indirect object** answers "to whom?" or "for whom?": She wrote *her grandmother* a letter.
- **Complement:** completes the meaning after a linking verb. He is *a doctor*. The soup tastes *salty*.
- **Adverbial (adjunct):** gives extra information about time, place, manner or reason. We met *at the station* *yesterday*.

> **Key idea:** English follows **Subject-Verb-Object (SVO)** word order: "Riya reads books", not "Riya books reads". Many Indian languages use Subject-Object-Verb order, so this is worth practising.

## The Five Basic Sentence Patterns

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| S + V | The baby slept. |
| S + V + O | The chef cooked dinner. |
| S + V + C | The chef is talented. |
| S + V + IO + DO | The chef gave us a recipe. |
| S + V + O + C | The chef made the kitchen spotless. |

Adverbials can be added to any pattern: "The baby slept *peacefully* *after lunch*."

## Phrases and Clauses

A **phrase** is a group of words without a subject-verb pair: *in the morning*, *the tall building*, *running quickly*. A phrase cannot stand alone as a sentence.

A **clause** is a group of words that has a subject and a verb.

- An **independent (main) clause** expresses a complete thought and can stand alone: *I finished my homework.*
- A **dependent (subordinate) clause** cannot stand alone; it depends on a main clause: *because I was tired*, *when the bell rang*, *who lives next door*.

### Types of Dependent Clauses

- **Adverb clauses** act like adverbs, answering when, why, how or under what condition: "*When the bell rang*, the students left."
- **Adjective (relative) clauses** describe a noun, usually starting with *who, whom, whose, which* or *that*: "The student *who topped the class* got a scholarship."
- **Noun clauses** act as nouns: "*What she said* surprised everyone." "I know *that you tried*."

### Defining and Non-Defining Relative Clauses

- A **defining** clause identifies which person or thing we mean, and has no commas: "Students *who submit late* lose marks." (Only those students.)
- A **non-defining** clause adds extra information about something already identified, and uses commas: "My brother, *who lives in Bengaluru*, is an engineer." (I have one brother; the clause just adds detail.)

Use *that* only in defining clauses. In non-defining clauses, use *which* for things and *who* for people.

## Types of Sentences by Structure

### Simple Sentences

One independent clause.

- "The train arrived late."
- "Riya and Meera studied and revised together." (compound subject and compound verb, but still one clause)

### Compound Sentences

Two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon.

- "The train arrived late, *so* we missed the meeting."
- "I wanted to go; however, it was raining."

### Complex Sentences

One independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

- "*Because the train arrived late*, we missed the meeting."
- "We missed the meeting *because the train arrived late*."

When the dependent clause comes first, put a comma after it. When it comes second, you usually do not need one.

### Compound-Complex Sentences

At least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

- "*Although the train was late*, we reached the venue, *and* the meeting started on time."

> **Key idea:** Good writing mixes sentence types. Too many short, simple sentences sound choppy; too many long, complex ones tire the reader. Vary them.

## Types of Sentences by Purpose

- **Declarative** makes a statement: "The library opens at nine."
- **Interrogative** asks a question: "When does the library open?"
- **Imperative** gives a command or request: "Please return the book by Friday." (The subject *you* is understood.)
- **Exclamatory** expresses strong feeling: "What a beautiful campus this is!"

### Forming Questions

- **Yes/no questions** move the auxiliary before the subject: "She is coming." becomes "Is she coming?" If there is no auxiliary, use *do/does/did*: "He plays chess." becomes "Does he play chess?" (not "Does he plays").
- **Wh- questions** start with a question word: "Where do you live?", "Why did she leave?"
- **Indirect questions** keep statement word order: "Can you tell me where the office *is*?", not "where is the office?"

## Active and Passive Voice

In the **active voice**, the subject performs the action: "The committee *approved* the proposal."

In the **passive voice**, the subject receives the action: "The proposal *was approved* by the committee."

The passive is formed with a form of *be* plus the past participle.

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | They clean the room. | The room is cleaned. |
| Simple past | They cleaned the room. | The room was cleaned. |
| Present continuous | They are cleaning the room. | The room is being cleaned. |
| Present perfect | They have cleaned the room. | The room has been cleaned. |
| Future | They will clean the room. | The room will be cleaned. |
| Modal | They must clean the room. | The room must be cleaned. |

Use the passive when the doer is unknown or unimportant ("My phone was stolen"), or in scientific and formal writing where the process matters more than the person ("The samples were heated to 100 degrees"). Otherwise, prefer the active voice, which is more direct and lively.

## Direct and Indirect Speech

**Direct speech** reports exact words in quotation marks: She said, "I am tired."

**Indirect (reported) speech** reports the meaning without quotation marks, usually shifting tenses back and changing pronouns and time words: She said that she *was* tired.

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| "I **am** busy," he said. | He said that he **was** busy. |
| "I **finished** it," she said. | She said that she **had finished** it. |
| "I **will** call," he said. | He said that he **would** call. |
| "**Can** you help?" she asked. | She asked if I **could** help. |
| "Close the door," the teacher said. | The teacher told us **to close** the door. |

Time and place words also change: *today* becomes *that day*, *tomorrow* becomes *the next day*, *here* becomes *there*. If the reported fact is still true, the tense need not change: "He said that the earth *moves* round the sun."

## Common Sentence Errors

### Fragments

A **fragment** is an incomplete sentence, missing a subject, a verb, or a main clause.

- Fragment: "Because I missed the bus."
- Fixed: "I was late because I missed the bus."

### Run-On Sentences and Comma Splices

A **run-on** joins independent clauses with no punctuation; a **comma splice** joins them with only a comma.

- Wrong: "I studied all night I still failed."
- Wrong: "I studied all night, I still failed."
- Fixed: "I studied all night, but I still failed." / "I studied all night; I still failed." / "Although I studied all night, I still failed."

### Misplaced and Dangling Modifiers

A **modifier** must sit next to the word it describes.

- Misplaced: "She *almost* drove her kids to school every day." (Did she almost drive?) Better: "She drove her kids to school *almost* every day."
- Dangling: "*Walking to college*, the rain started." (The rain was not walking.) Better: "*Walking to college*, I got caught in the rain."

### Faulty Parallelism

Items in a list or comparison should share the same grammatical form.

- Wrong: "She likes reading, to swim and painting."
- Right: "She likes reading, swimming and painting."

## Summary

- A sentence needs a subject and a predicate and expresses a complete thought.
- English uses Subject-Verb-Object order; five basic patterns build most sentences.
- Phrases lack a subject-verb pair; independent clauses stand alone; dependent clauses do not.
- Sentences can be simple, compound, complex or compound-complex, and declarative, interrogative, imperative or exclamatory.
- Prefer the active voice; use the passive when the doer is unknown or unimportant.
- Reported speech usually shifts tenses, pronouns and time words.
- Avoid fragments, run-ons, comma splices, dangling modifiers and faulty parallelism.

## Practice Questions

1. Identify the subject, verb and object: "The manager sent the team an email."
2. Classify as simple, compound, complex or compound-complex: "When the results came, Asha smiled, but Rohan looked worried."
3. Convert to passive: "The students are organising a fest."
4. Convert to indirect speech: He said, "I will submit the report tomorrow."
5. Fix the error: "Having finished the exam, the hall was left quietly."
