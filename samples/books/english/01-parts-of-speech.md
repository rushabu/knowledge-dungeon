# Parts of Speech

Every English sentence is built from words, and every word does a particular job. Some name things, some describe them, some show action and some connect ideas. These jobs are called **parts of speech**. Knowing them is like knowing the pieces in chess: once you understand what each piece does, you can see how sentences work and fix them when they go wrong.

English has eight traditional parts of speech: nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections. Many grammar books also treat **articles** and **determiners** as a separate group.

> **Key idea:** A word's part of speech depends on how it is used in a sentence, not on the word alone. "Light" is a noun in "Switch on the light", a verb in "Light the lamp", and an adjective in "a light bag".

## Nouns

A **noun** names a person, place, thing, animal, idea or feeling: *teacher*, *Mumbai*, *laptop*, *tiger*, *freedom*, *happiness*.

### Kinds of Nouns

- **Common nouns** name general things: *city*, *river*, *student*.
- **Proper nouns** name particular things and begin with a capital letter: *Delhi*, *the Ganga*, *Priya*.
- **Concrete nouns** name things you can see or touch: *book*, *chair*.
- **Abstract nouns** name ideas and qualities: *honesty*, *courage*, *knowledge*.
- **Collective nouns** name groups: *a team of players*, *a flock of birds*, *a bunch of keys*.
- **Countable nouns** can be counted and have plural forms: *one pen, two pens*.
- **Uncountable nouns** cannot be counted directly and have no plural: *water*, *information*, *advice*, *furniture*, *luggage*.

A very common mistake among Indian English speakers is pluralising uncountable nouns. We say "some advice", not "advices"; "a lot of information", not "informations"; "pieces of furniture", not "furnitures".

### Plural Forms

- Most nouns add *-s*: *book, books*.
- Nouns ending in *-s, -sh, -ch, -x* add *-es*: *bus, buses*; *box, boxes*.
- Consonant + *y* becomes *-ies*: *city, cities*. (But vowel + *y* just adds *-s*: *day, days*.)
- Some nouns ending in *-f* or *-fe* change to *-ves*: *knife, knives*; *leaf, leaves*.
- Irregular plurals must be learnt: *child, children*; *man, men*; *tooth, teeth*; *mouse, mice*; *criterion, criteria*; *phenomenon, phenomena*.

### Possessive Nouns

Add *'s* to show ownership: *Ravi's bike*, *the teacher's desk*. For plural nouns ending in *s*, add only an apostrophe: *the students' results*. For irregular plurals, add *'s*: *children's books*.

## Pronouns

A **pronoun** replaces a noun so we do not repeat it. "Meera lost Meera's phone" becomes "Meera lost her phone".

| Type | Examples | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Personal (subject) | I, you, he, she, it, we, they | Doer of the action: *She sings.* |
| Personal (object) | me, you, him, her, it, us, them | Receiver: *Call me.* |
| Possessive | mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs | Ownership: *The bag is mine.* |
| Reflexive | myself, yourself, himself, themselves | Action returns to the subject: *He hurt himself.* |
| Demonstrative | this, that, these, those | Pointing: *This is my seat.* |
| Interrogative | who, whom, whose, which, what | Questions: *Who called?* |
| Relative | who, whom, whose, which, that | Joining clauses: *The girl who won...* |
| Indefinite | someone, anyone, everyone, nobody, each | Non-specific: *Everyone agreed.* |

### Common Pronoun Errors

- **Subject vs object:** "Rahul and *I* went" (subject), but "The teacher praised Rahul and *me*" (object). Test by removing the other person: you would say "praised me", not "praised I".
- **Reflexive misuse:** "Please contact myself" is wrong. Use "Please contact me". Reflexives are only for actions that return to the subject, or for emphasis: "I made it *myself*."
- **Vague reference:** "Riya told Priya that she had passed." Who passed? Rewrite to make it clear.

## Verbs

A **verb** expresses an action (*run*, *write*, *think*) or a state of being (*is*, *seem*, *belong*). Every complete sentence needs a verb.

- **Action verbs** show what someone does: *She writes code.*
- **Linking verbs** connect the subject to a description: *He is tired. The food smells good.* Common linking verbs are forms of *be*, and *seem*, *become*, *look*, *feel*, *taste*.
- **Auxiliary (helping) verbs** help the main verb form tenses, questions and negatives: *be*, *have*, *do*.
- **Modal verbs** express ability, permission, possibility or obligation: *can*, *could*, *may*, *might*, *must*, *shall*, *should*, *will*, *would*. They are always followed by the base form: "She *can speak*", not "She can speaks".

Verbs can be **transitive** (they need an object: "She *bought* a phone") or **intransitive** (no object: "The baby *slept*").

Verbs change form to show tense, which is the subject of Chapter 3.

## Adjectives

An **adjective** describes a noun or pronoun: *a tall building*, *three students*, *an interesting book*, *She is happy*.

### Degrees of Comparison

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| tall | taller | tallest |
| happy | happier | happiest |
| beautiful | more beautiful | most beautiful |
| good | better | best |
| bad | worse | worst |
| little | less | least |

Use the comparative to compare two things ("Ravi is *taller than* Aman") and the superlative for three or more ("Ravi is *the tallest* in the class"). Never double them: "more better" and "most tallest" are wrong.

### Order of Adjectives

When several adjectives describe one noun, native speakers follow an order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose. So we say "a *lovely little old round red Indian wooden* box", not "a red wooden little lovely box". In practice, two or three adjectives are plenty.

## Adverbs

An **adverb** modifies a verb, an adjective or another adverb. It answers *how*, *when*, *where*, *how often* or *to what extent*.

- How: *She spoke clearly.*
- When: *We will leave tomorrow.*
- Where: *Please sit here.*
- How often: *He always arrives late.*
- To what extent: *The test was very difficult.*

Many adverbs end in *-ly* (*quickly*, *carefully*), but not all (*fast*, *well*, *soon*, *often*). Remember: *good* is an adjective and *well* is the adverb. "She sings *well*", not "She sings good".

## Prepositions

A **preposition** shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another word, often of place, time or direction: *in*, *on*, *at*, *under*, *between*, *through*, *during*, *since*, *for*.

### In, On and At for Time and Place

| | Time | Place |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | exact times: *at 5 pm, at noon* | specific points: *at the gate, at the bus stop* |
| **on** | days and dates: *on Monday, on 15 August* | surfaces and streets: *on the table, on MG Road* |
| **in** | months, years, seasons, parts of the day: *in May, in 2026, in the morning* | enclosed spaces, cities, countries: *in the room, in Pune* |

### Common Preposition Mistakes

- "Discuss *about* the plan" is wrong: *discuss* already means "talk about". Say "discuss the plan".
- "Married *with*" is wrong: say "married *to*".
- "Since two years" is wrong: *since* takes a starting point (*since 2024*), *for* takes a duration (*for two years*).
- "Return back" and "revert back" repeat the idea; say "return" or "reply".

## Conjunctions

A **conjunction** joins words, phrases or clauses.

- **Coordinating conjunctions** join equal parts: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so* (remember **FANBOYS**). "I was tired, *but* I finished the assignment."
- **Subordinating conjunctions** join a dependent clause to a main clause: *because, although, if, when, since, unless, while, after, before*. "*Although* it rained, the match continued."
- **Correlative conjunctions** work in pairs: *either...or*, *neither...nor*, *both...and*, *not only...but also*.

## Interjections

An **interjection** expresses sudden feeling: *Wow!*, *Oh!*, *Oops!*, *Hurray!* Interjections are common in speech and informal writing but should be avoided in formal writing.

## Articles and Determiners

**Articles** are a small but tricky group: *a*, *an* and *the*. They are a kind of **determiner**, a word that introduces a noun and tells us which one or how many (*this*, *my*, *some*, *every*, *few*). Articles are covered in detail in Chapter 4.

## Identifying Parts of Speech: A Worked Example

"Wow, the young engineer quickly solved the difficult problem because she understood it well."

- *Wow*: interjection
- *the*: article
- *young*: adjective
- *engineer*: noun
- *quickly*: adverb
- *solved*: verb
- *difficult*: adjective
- *problem*: noun
- *because*: subordinating conjunction
- *she*: pronoun
- *understood*: verb
- *it*: pronoun
- *well*: adverb

## Summary

- Nouns name things; watch uncountable nouns like *advice*, *information* and *furniture*.
- Pronouns replace nouns; use subject forms (*I, he*) and object forms (*me, him*) correctly, and avoid misusing *myself*.
- Verbs show action or state; modals are followed by the base form.
- Adjectives describe nouns and have comparative and superlative forms; adverbs describe verbs, adjectives and other adverbs.
- Prepositions show relationships; learn *in*, *on* and *at*, and *since* versus *for*.
- Conjunctions join ideas; interjections express emotion.
- A word's part of speech depends on its use in the sentence.

## Practice Questions

1. Identify the part of speech of each word: "The old man walked slowly across the busy road."
2. Correct the errors: "She gave me many useful advices and informations."
3. Fill in with *in*, *on* or *at*: "The exam is ___ Monday ___ 10 am ___ the main hall."
4. Write the comparative and superlative of *far*, *easy*, *careful* and *bad*.
5. Rewrite to remove the ambiguous pronoun: "When Arjun met Kabir, he was nervous."
