# Formal Letters, Emails and Workplace Writing

At college and at work, much of your communication is written: emails to professors, internship applications, complaint letters, meeting minutes and reports. These documents are judged quickly, often by busy readers, and they shape how people see you. A clear, polite, well-organised email can open doors; a careless one can close them. This chapter covers the formats and etiquette of formal written communication.

## Principles of Professional Writing

Remember the **7 Cs** of business communication. Good messages are:

1. **Clear:** the purpose is obvious and the language simple.
2. **Concise:** no unnecessary words or repetition.
3. **Concrete:** specific facts, figures and dates rather than vague statements.
4. **Correct:** accurate information, grammar, spelling and names.
5. **Coherent:** ideas flow logically.
6. **Complete:** the reader has everything needed to understand and act.
7. **Courteous:** polite and respectful, even when complaining or refusing.

> **Key idea:** Before writing, ask: Who is my reader? What do I want them to know or do? Then put the most important point first.

## Formal Letters

Formal letters are still used for official applications, complaints, legal matters and correspondence with government offices.

### Layout (Block Format)

In the modern **block format**, everything is aligned to the left.

1. **Sender's address** (and optionally phone and email)
2. **Date:** *26 September 2026*
3. **Receiver's designation and address:** *The Principal, ABC College of Engineering, Pune 411001*
4. **Subject line:** a short statement of purpose, often in bold: *Subject: Request for a bonafide certificate*
5. **Salutation:** *Dear Sir/Madam,* or *Dear Dr Sharma,* (use the name if you know it)
6. **Body:**
   - *Opening paragraph:* state who you are and why you are writing.
   - *Middle paragraph(s):* give details, reasons and supporting information.
   - *Closing paragraph:* state what action you request and thank the reader.
7. **Complimentary close:** *Yours faithfully,* (when the salutation is "Dear Sir/Madam") or *Yours sincerely,* (when you used the person's name)
8. **Signature and name**, with designation or roll number if relevant
9. **Enclosures:** *Encl: Copy of fee receipt*

### Sample Letter of Request

```
Riya Sharma
B-12, Green Park Society
Pune 411038

26 September 2026

The Principal
ABC College of Engineering
Pune 411001

Subject: Request for a bonafide certificate

Dear Sir/Madam,

I am a third-year student of Computer Engineering (Roll No. 21CE045).
I am writing to request a bonafide certificate, which I need to apply
for an educational loan from State Bank of India.

I have attached a copy of my college ID card and the bank's letter.
I would be grateful if the certificate could be issued by 5 October 2026,
as the loan application closes on 8 October.

Thank you for your time and assistance.

Yours faithfully,
Riya Sharma

Encl: (1) Copy of ID card (2) Bank's letter
```

### Types of Formal Letters

- **Letter of request / application:** leave, certificates, permissions.
- **Complaint letter:** state the problem factually, give details (dates, order numbers), describe the impact, and request a specific solution. Stay polite and firm.
- **Letter to the editor:** express an opinion on a public issue, with reasons and suggestions.
- **Cover letter:** accompanies a job or internship application (see below).
- **Letter of enquiry:** requests information, such as about a course or product.

## Emails

Email is the main written channel in most workplaces and colleges. Its informality in speed does not mean informality in style.

### Parts of a Professional Email

1. **Subject line:** specific and short. "Request to reschedule DBMS viva (Roll No. 21CE045)" is far better than "Hi" or "Urgent".
2. **Salutation:**
   - Formal: *Dear Professor Kulkarni,* / *Dear Ms Mehta,*
   - Neutral: *Hello Mr Singh,*
   - Avoid *Hey*, *Hi Sir* and *Respected Sir* in international or modern professional contexts; *Dear Sir/Madam* is fine when you do not know the name.
3. **Opening line:** state your purpose immediately. "I am writing to ask whether..." Introduce yourself if the reader does not know you.
4. **Body:** short paragraphs, one idea each. Use bullet points for several items or questions.
5. **Call to action:** state clearly what you need and by when.
6. **Closing:** *Best regards,* / *Kind regards,* / *Thank you,* followed by your full name and relevant details (course, roll number, phone).

### Sample Email to a Professor

```
Subject: Request for extension - ML assignment 2 (Roll No. 21CE045)

Dear Professor Kulkarni,

I am writing to request a two-day extension for Machine Learning
Assignment 2, which is due on Friday, 3 October.

I was admitted to hospital from 28 to 30 September with a viral fever,
and I have attached the discharge summary. I have completed the data
preprocessing and model training sections, and I need a little more
time to finish the evaluation and report.

Would it be possible to submit the assignment by Sunday, 5 October?
I would be grateful for your consideration.

Kind regards,
Riya Sharma
TE Computer Engineering, Division A
```

### Email Etiquette

- **Reply promptly,** ideally within one working day, even if only to acknowledge.
- **Keep it short:** if an email needs scrolling, consider whether a call or meeting would be better.
- **Use *Reply all* carefully,** only when everyone needs your response.
- **Use CC and BCC correctly:** *To* is for people who must act; *CC* for people who should be informed; *BCC* hides recipients from each other (useful for large mailing lists).
- **Attach files properly** and mention them in the text. Check that the attachment is really there before sending.
- **Avoid** all capitals (it reads as shouting), text-speak (*pls, u, thx*), excessive exclamation marks and emojis in formal emails.
- **Proofread** names, dates, figures and attachments before clicking send.
- **Never write in anger.** Draft, wait, and reread before sending a sensitive message.
- Remember that emails are **permanent** and can be forwarded. Do not write anything you would not want seen by others.

> **Key idea:** A good subject line and a clear first sentence let a busy reader understand your email in five seconds.

## Cover Letters

A **cover letter** introduces you when applying for a job or internship. It should be tailored to each position and fit on one page.

1. **Opening:** the position you are applying for, where you saw it, and a one-line hook about why you are a strong fit.
2. **Middle (one or two paragraphs):** your most relevant skills and achievements, with evidence. "I built a Flask web app used by 300 students for timetable management" is stronger than "I have good programming skills."
3. **Why this organisation:** show that you have researched them.
4. **Closing:** express enthusiasm, mention your attached resume, and request an interview.

Match your language to the job description, and never send the same generic letter to every company.

## Resumes in Brief

A resume (or CV) summarises your qualifications. For students:

- **Contact details:** name, phone, professional email, LinkedIn or GitHub.
- **Education:** degree, college, year, CGPA.
- **Skills:** technical and relevant soft skills.
- **Projects and internships:** what you did, tools used, and results, starting each point with an **action verb** (*developed, designed, analysed, led, improved*).
- **Achievements and activities:** competitions, certifications, positions of responsibility.

Keep it to one page, use a clean layout, and make sure every claim can be discussed in an interview.

## Other Workplace Documents

### Memos

A **memo** is a short internal document for announcements or instructions within an organisation. It has headings (*To, From, Date, Subject*) and a brief, direct body. Today, many memos are sent as emails.

### Notices

A **notice** announces an event or information to a group, often on a notice board or website. It includes the organisation's name, the word NOTICE, a date, a clear heading, the essential details (what, when, where, who) and the issuer's name and designation. It is usually placed in a box and kept brief.

### Minutes of Meetings

**Minutes** record what happened in a meeting: date, time, place, attendees, apologies, each agenda item, key discussions, **decisions made** and **action items** (who will do what by when). They are written in the past tense and in neutral language.

### Reports

A formal **report** presents information and analysis for decision-making. A typical structure is: title page, executive summary, introduction (purpose and scope), methodology, findings, conclusions, recommendations and appendices. Use headings, numbered sections and tables so readers can find information quickly.

## Summary

- Professional messages follow the 7 Cs: clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete and courteous.
- Formal letters use block format with addresses, date, subject, salutation, a three-part body, a matching close and signature.
- Professional emails need a specific subject line, an appropriate salutation, a clear purpose, short paragraphs, a call to action and a proper sign-off.
- Follow email etiquette: reply promptly, use CC and BCC correctly, check attachments, avoid text-speak and never write in anger.
- Cover letters and resumes should be tailored, evidence-based and concise.
- Memos, notices, minutes and reports each have standard formats.
